Rising now

The words gaining ground fastest as a share of everything arXiv publishes. Each one is ranked by how much growth we can be confident in rather than by its headline number, and every candidate word is tested together so that a few winners cannot turn up by chance alone. One paper becoming five is not a breakout.

When each idea broke out

Each bar marks a stretch when a word ran persistently above its own long-run rate, found using Kleinberg's burst detection. You get a start date and an end date rather than a shape to squint at. Thicker bars burned hotter, and the label on the right is the field the word mostly belongs to. Words that show up evenly across every field are left out, which is what keeps ordinary academic phrasing off the list. Click any row to chart it.

The deadline fingerprint

Conference deadlines leave a mark on when a field posts. The dashed circle is an average month, and each lobe shows how far above or below that a given month runs. Fields built around big annual conferences come out lopsided. Fields without them stay close to round.

How this is built

A paper is counted in the month it was first posted, never the month it was last edited, so revising an old paper cannot move it into the present. A paper counts in every field it is listed under, which is what arXiv's own cat: search does, so the numbers here should match what you find there. Shares always divide by the total papers in the same snapshot. They never divide by another source, and never by whichever lines you happen to have selected.

Three things this data honestly cannot tell you. Fields are recorded as they stand today, so a 2010 paper added to machine learning in 2024 still counts as machine learning back in 2010, and today's fashions get painted onto the past. Titles and abstracts are the latest version, so a 2016 paper revised in 2025 can appear to use a 2025 word in 2016. And arXiv is not all of science. Posting preprints is close to universal in high energy physics and rare in chemistry and clinical medicine, so comparing across fields partly measures posting habits rather than research.
